Chapter I · 1888 – 1902

Roots: Tamil Nadu & the Andhra Coast

Long before laboratory benches, Raman's eye was shaped by a grandmother's puja lamps, his father's violin, and iridescent shells on a Vizag beach.

Birth

7 November 1888

Tiruvanaikkaval, near TrichyFirst breath

Raman is born at his mother Parvathi's parental home near the great Jambukeswarar temple. His father teaches at SPG College on ten rupees a month.

Both Parvathi's confinements were at this house near the Kaveri river. The Tamil calendar records the exact moment: Sarvadhari year, twenty-fourth day of Aippasi, twenty-seven naligais of Wednesday, second padam of Purada, Tulam lagnam. Family ancestry runs back to Vaigalathur near Thanjavur.

Jambukeswarar TempleKaveri riverbankSPG College, Trichinopoly
Childhood

1897

Swamimalai, near KumbakonamThe paal-kaavadi journey

Nine-year-old Raman and brother CS carry the paal-kaavadi to the Subrahmanya temple for their upanayanam — a ceremony costing 500 rupees, six months of their father's salary.

The kaavadi is a wooden bar on the shoulder with pots of milk on each side, brass bells announcing the bearer. Raman's grandfather had walked to this temple from Mangudi and Porasakudi regularly. Though Chandrasekhara Aiyar had become an agnostic student in Madras, the ceremony was arranged. Raman later laughed: "Six months' salary to have us carry that paal-kaavadi, whew!"

Subrahmanya Temple, Swamimalai hill
Childhood

1892 – 1902

Visakhapatnam — Waltair quarterSchoolboy by the sea

Father joins Mrs. A.V.N. College. Raman grows up in Waltair, always youngest and best in class. He studies iridescent shells on the beach and asks his first optics questions. Matriculates at twelve.

Father takes the boys to Dolphin's Nose — the rocky promontory with its panoramic view over the Bay of Bengal — for real-world lessons in physiography. They also visit the Borra Caves in the Ananthagiri hills, stalactite formations that kindle Raman's curiosity about how mineral shapes form. On the Vizag beach, Raman explains to younger Skandan how shells produce colours: thin crystal layers of calcium carbonate stacked like interference films — the same physics he will spend his life on. He reads Maxwell's Electromagnetic Theory from his father's top shelf and finishes it in a day. Maxwell is his first hero.

Chapter II · 1903 – 1907

Madras — Presidency College

A fourteen-year-old arrives in a city of sea-breezes and second-hand journals. Professors assume he's in the wrong classroom. He isn't.

Education

1903 – 1907

Madras — Presidency CollegeFourteen, already beyond the syllabus

Professor Elliott assumes the slight boy is in the wrong room. He isn't. Raman wins gold medals in physics and English, earns his MSc at eighteen. Professor Llewellyn Jones grants him carte blanche on the labs at any hour.

Every morning, dawn walks on the Marina Beach — watching fishermen push catamarans into the surf, noting the colours of the eastern horizon. Afternoons at the Connemara Library (stocked from Haileybury College's donation), reading everything from Homer to the Philosophical Magazine. For harder-to-find journals he goes to Moore Market — second-hand stalls stacked to the ceiling, English officers' discarded science journals. He finds several issues of the Philosophical Magazine here and decides this is where he'll send his papers. The family settles at 52 Pycrofts Road, Triplicane, run by great-aunt Muthuchipi who feeds him rice-kanji at dawn. The civil surgeon bars him from England — a verdict Raman will call the greatest favour of his life. His wedding to Lokasundari is held in 1907 at the Maharajah of Vizianagaram Girls' School premises, Triplicane — reported in The Hindu as a landmark inter-sect alliance.

Chapter III · 1907 – 1933

Calcutta — The Double Life

By day, counting the Crown's coins at Dalhousie Square. By night, a physicist at 210 Bow Bazar Street who would change how humanity understands light.

Double Life

1907 – 1917

Calcutta — Bow Bazar & IACS"It won't be desolate now. I am here."

On a tram to Dalhousie Square, Raman spots a board: "Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science." He jumps off at the next stop. An empty, well-equipped lab at 210 Bow Bazar Street. His exile is over.

Raman joins the Finance Service; Bow Bazar is crowded, there's an open sewer behind the house, but it's within half a mile of IACS. By 1915 he takes house 1/5/1B Bow Bazar Street, back-to-back with the lab — the IACS compound wall has a door cut in it, and Raman has the key. Office is at Dalhousie Square; tram each way, never a minute late, never a minute extra. Evenings: walks on the Maidan with Lokam, him discussing the office, her listening. Sundays: a phaeton on Chowringhee to savour the flowering trees. Ashu Babu (Asutosh Dey), the IACS lab assistant, becomes Raman's staunchest supporter for twenty-five years.

IACS, 210 Bow Bazar St1/5/1B Bow Bazar (home 1915-33)Dalhousie SquareThe MaidanChowringheeKalitola tram stop
Palit Chair · Discovery

1917 – 1933

University of Calcutta — Palit ChairCaesar paid off. Science unchained.

Sir Asutosh offers the Palit Professorship; Raman resigns from the Finance Service the same day. On 28 February 1928, at the IACS lab, the Raman Effect is proved. A one-page letter to Nature. 150 citations within a year.

The University College of Science at Rajabazar and the IACS become twin centres of world-class research. Raman trains 500 students over sixteen years. He spots "ten-kilowatt brains" on first meeting. In July 1930, four months before the Nobel Committee announces their decision, Raman quietly books tickets to Stockholm. He knows. India celebrates 28 February as National Science Day.

Chapter IV · 1909 – 1912

Postings: Rangoon & Nagpur

The government sends him away. He sets up laboratories in upstairs rooms and keeps working.

Govt Service

1909 – 1910

Rangoon, BurmaExile across the Bay

Promoted to Currency Officer, Raman is posted across the Bay of Bengal. He carries his notebooks and the address of N.C. Krishna Iyer at Rangoon College. His mother is horrified.

The lack of instruments drives Raman to insomnia — night after night he paces the floor. The sky over Rangoon is spectacular on clear nights; he dreams of a telescope for Halley's Comet. On Sundays, he and Lokam take a boat on Royal Lake — 160 acres of water, 205 acres of parkland — at the exorbitant cost of a 15-rupee taxi. They visit a sawmill to watch elephants pull teak logs from the river. Father dies in February 1910, aged 44. Raman boards a freighter to Vizagapatam on the eleventh day.

Currency OfficeRangoon CollegeRoyal Lake / Dalhousie ParkTeak sawmill on river
Govt Service

1910 – 1912

Nagpur, Central ProvincesThe upstairs laboratory

Posted as Deputy Accountant General. Raman surveys the city, picks a house near Sitabuldi Square within walking distance of Science College — and turns the large upstairs room into a lab the same week.

He refuses the grand Civil Lines bungalow — too far from Science College, no trams, no tongas. He picks the first house between Sitabuldi Square and Chota Dhantoli: mud walls, outer brick, a large room upstairs, a veranda overlooking a maidan and the Buty garden. Instruments spread on large and small tables. Papers on acoustics of bowed strings — the wolf note, how the bridge in Lokam's veena differs from a violin — appear in European journals citing "Nagpur" as the institution. Science needs curiosity more than expensive equipment.

Chapter V · 1921 – 1925

The Question on the Open Sea

London, a cathedral, a Nicol prism, and a blue Mediterranean. The question asked mid-ocean takes seven years to prove — and changes physics forever.

First Voyage

Summer 1921

London — St Paul's CathedralWhispering Gallery acoustics

Raman's first trip outside India. He visits St Paul's Cathedral, studies the Whispering Gallery under the dome, and co-publishes an acoustics paper in Nature that same year.

The Whispering Gallery allows a whisper on one side of the dome to travel 34 metres around the curved wall to the opposite side. Raman and his co-author explain this as a guided wave, not simple reflection — the paper appears in Nature. He also visits the Royal Society at Burlington House, Piccadilly, and meets leading British physicists who already know his name from the journals. They treat him with immediate respect. He is representing Calcutta University at the Universities Congress.

St Paul's CathedralRoyal Society, Burlington House
Eureka

Return voyage, 1921

The Mediterranean SeaWhy is the sea blue?

Sailing home, Raman is transfixed by the deep blue opalescence. He produces a Nicol prism, quenches the sky's reflection — the water grows bluer still. Lord Rayleigh said it was merely reflected sky. Raman proves it isn't.

Water molecules themselves scatter light inelastically — the seeds of the Raman Effect, seven years before it is formally proved. He writes up the observation before the ship docks. Papers appear in the Proceedings of the Royal Society and the Journal of the Optical Society of America within months. A single Nicol prism on a ship deck. No lab required. Just the right question, and the nerve to test it mid-ocean.

North America

1924

Pasadena, California — CaltechFRS & four months in the California sun

Elected Fellow of the Royal Society (only the fourth Indian), Raman tours North America: the British Association in Toronto, then four months as Millikan's guest at Caltech.

At the Franklin Institute centenary in Philadelphia, Millikan is immediately struck by Raman's range. He invites him to Pasadena for four months. Raman lectures, builds a global network, and returns to Calcutta carrying not hope but certainty — that the Nobel Prize is coming, and which experiment will win it.

Caltech campus, PasadenaBritish Association, TorontoFranklin Institute, Philadelphia
"A voyage to Europe in the summer of 1921 gave me the first opportunity of observing the wonderful blue opalescence of the Mediterranean Sea."
— C.V. Raman · Nobel Lecture · Stockholm · 11 December 1930

Chapter VI · 1928 – 1931

Discovery & the Nobel Route

Through Mysore, Bombay, Egypt, Rome, Genoa, Bristol — to the Nobel stage in Stockholm where India had no flag.

Discovery

28 February 1928

IACS Laboratory, Bow BazarA new type of secondary radiation

Raman and K.S. Krishnan observe that scattered photons carry a shifted wavelength. One-page letter to Nature, published 31 March 1928. India marks this day as National Science Day.

Nobel Route

1929

Mysore — the DurbarThe gold-brocaded coat

The Maharaja confers the title "Rajasabha Bhushana" with a gold-brocaded coat and shawl. Raman treasures this robe more than any British knighthood and keeps the photograph of himself in durbar regalia all his life.

Nobel Route

1929

Bombay — Apollo BunderSetting sail

Raman and Lokasundari board ship at Apollo Bunder. Stockholm tickets already booked. The Nobel announcement is still months away.

Nobel Route

En route, 1929

Egypt — Suez & CairoPyramids on the way to the Prize

Through the Suez Canal to Cairo. Raman visits the Pyramids at Giza — seeing in the ancient Egyptians a kindred obsession with geometry and the behaviour of light and shadow.

Nobel Route

1929

Rome, ItalyMuseums & ancient glass

At the Vatican galleries, Raman notices a Syrian glass vase — its iridescent surface is lamellar interference, the same phenomenon he is studying. He arranges for flakes to be sent to Bangalore for analysis later.

Nobel Route

1929

GenoaGateway to the north

From Genoa's harbour, northward. The Ligurian coast frames the same blue-green Mediterranean that captivated him in 1921 — the question posed on that return voyage is now the answer that wins the Prize.

Nobel Route

1929

Bristol, EnglandFaraday Society address

At the Faraday Society meeting, Raman delivers his most comprehensive public exposition of the Raman Effect. The room is electrified. Stockholm cannot be far.

Nobel Prize

10 December 1930

Stockholm, SwedenFirst Asian Nobel in science

King Gustav V places the medal in Raman's hands. As applause fills the Concert Hall, he sees the British Union Jack above his seat. India has no flag. He weeps. He is forty-two.

At the Nobel banquet in Stockholm City Hall, Raman tells the assembled laureates that the greatest obstacle to science in his country is poverty — and the greatest resource is the curiosity of its people. He is the first Asian, the first person from outside Europe or North America, to win the Nobel Prize in Physics. After the ceremony he travels to Uppsala as the guest of Manne Siegbahn before sailing home to an India that receives him with tremendous celebration.

Chapter VII · 1933 – 1970

Bangalore — Garden City, Final Laboratory

He planted trees on a campus, visited diamond mines in Panna, refused government money, and built the last and greatest institution of his life.

Institution Builder

1933 – 1948

Indian Institute of Science, BangaloreThe director plants trees

His first act as director: order saplings planted across the campus. Those trees are now broad avenues. He founds the Indian Academy of Sciences in 1934. No government grants. Not a paisa.

Ousted from the directorship by academic politics in 1937, Raman continues as Professor of Physics at IISc for eleven more years. He studies diamond lattices, the optics of crystals, and the iridescence of ancient Syrian glass. He snatches G.N. Ramachandran from the electrical engineering department: "He's a bit too bright to be in yours." Ramachandran becomes one of India's finest scientific minds. The Indian Academy of Sciences meets in small university towns across India to "galvanize the younger generation towards science."

IISc main campusPhysics dept, IIScDirector's bungalowIndian Academy of Sciences
Research Journey

1942

Panna, Madhya PradeshInto the diamond fields

Wartime India. Raman travels to Panna's diamond mines on the Vindhyan plateau to study gem diamonds at their source — feeding directly into his work on diamond lattice vibrations.

Legacy

1948 – 21 November 1970

Raman Research Institute, Bangalore"There will be others to enjoy them."

Raman founds and funds his own institute — no government money. A few days before his death, aged 82, he plants a papaya tree in the garden: "I will not be here when it bears fruit, but there will be others."

Chapter VIII · 1958

The Lenin Prize Tour

Bombay · Genoa · Gothenburg · Moscow · Budapest. His last great international journey.

Late Honour

1958

Moscow, USSRThe Lenin Peace Prize

At seventy, Raman receives the Lenin Peace Prize and addresses the Soviet Academy of Sciences. He refuses to be used as a political symbol. He speaks of scientists' responsibility to resist the weaponisation of their work.

Late Honour

1958

Budapest, HungaryHungarian Academy fellowship

The Hungarian Academy of Sciences confers a fellowship. Raman's final European stop. He returns to Bangalore and to the rose bushes he loves.

"A voyage to Europe gave me the first opportunity of observing the wonderful blue opalescence of the Mediterranean Sea — the same phenomenon that led, seven years later, to the discovery that bears my name."

— C.V. Raman · Nobel Lecture · Stockholm · 11 December 1930
