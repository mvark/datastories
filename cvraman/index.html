1888 – 1903

Roots: Tamil Nadu & the Andhra Coast

Long before laboratory benches and ocean voyages, C.V. Raman's scientific eye was shaped by a grandmother's puja lamps, a father's violin, and the iridescent shells on a sea-coast beach.

Childhood

7 November 1888

Tiruvanaikkaval, TrichyBirth

Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman is born at his mother Parvathi's parental home near the great Jambukeswarar temple. His father, Chandrasekhara Aiyar, is a student-teacher at SPG College earning ten rupees a month — a copper spoon, not silver, as Raman would later joke.

The family ancestry reaches back to Vaigalathur near Thanjavur. The boy born here will one day stand before a Swedish king.

Childhood

1897

Swamimalai, near KumbakonamThe upanayanam journey

Nine-year-old Raman and his elder brother CS travel to the Subrahmanya temple at Swamimalai — the family's sacred shrine — to perform the upanayanam ceremony. Carrying the paal-kaavadi through the town, the initiation cost 500 rupees — six months of their father's salary.

Raman's grandfather had walked to this temple regularly from the villages of Mangudi and Porasakudi, tending his land and his faith.

Childhood

1892 – 1902

Visakhapatnam (Waltair)Schoolboy by the sea

Chandrasekhara Aiyar joins Mrs. A.V.N. College and moves the family to Waltair — the British quarter of Vizagapatam — after a journey of more than two weeks by train, construction trains, canal boats, and bullock carts. Raman grows up here, always the youngest and best in his class.

His father takes the boys to Dolphin's Nose and the Borra Caves for lessons in physiography. Raman falls in love with seashells, their colours of calcium carbonate stacked into iridescent layers, and soap-bubble surface tension. He matriculates at twelve.

Education

1903 – 1907

Madras — Presidency CollegeFourteen, and already beyond the syllabus

Raman arrives at Presidency College at fourteen. Professor Elliott assumes the slight boy has walked into the wrong classroom. He hasn't. He wins gold medals in physics and English and earns his MSc by 1907. Professor R. Llewellyn Jones grants him carte blanche to work in the lab any hour he likes.

He walks the Triplicane beach at dawn, browses Moore Market for second-hand scientific journals, and reads everything in the Connemara Library. His first paper in the Philosophical Magazine is published when he is still an undergraduate. The civil surgeon bars him from England — a verdict Raman will later call the greatest favour of his life.

1907 – 1917

The Civil Servant Who Kept a Laboratory

Government postings moved Raman across India. Each city became an unofficial research station — a room upstairs, an improvised bench, and a question that couldn't wait until morning.

Government Service

1907 – 1909

Calcutta (Kolkata)A civil servant finds his laboratory

Raman joins the Indian Finance Service as Assistant Accountant General. Within weeks he discovers the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science (IACS) in Bowbazar — a neglected but fully equipped laboratory. He works in the office from ten to five; the rest of his waking hours belong to science.

He studies the acoustics of the mridangam and tabla, the physics of bowed strings, and the vibrations of the violin his father once played. The IACS director gives him his own key.

Government Service

1909 – 1911

Rangoon, BurmaCurrency Officer across the Bay

A posting as Currency Officer takes Raman across the Bay of Bengal to Rangoon. He carries his scientific notebooks with him. The administrative work is conscientious; the scientific correspondence with Calcutta never stops.

The transfer demonstrates what his brother CS had predicted — government service was a Kamadhenu, the mythical cow of plenty, funding the freedom to think. Raman returns to Calcutta at the first opportunity.

Government Service

1911 – 1913

Nagpur, Central ProvincesVibrating strings in an upstairs room

Posted to Nagpur as Deputy Accountant General, Raman sets up a small home laboratory in an upstairs room and continues his studies on vibrating strings and bowed instruments. Every weekend he experiments; every Monday he is back at his desk.

The improvised Nagpur laboratory produces papers on the acoustics of stringed instruments that European physicists will later cite. Science, Raman is proving, needs curiosity more than it needs expensive equipment.

Research

1917 – 1933

Calcutta — Palit ChairCaesar gets his due — and not a paisa more

Sir Asutosh Mookerjee offers Raman the Palit Professorship at the University of Calcutta. Raman resigns from the Indian Finance Service the same day — a salary cut of more than half, accepted without hesitation.

For the next sixteen years Calcutta is his world: the IACS laboratory, the university lecture hall, five hundred students. Here the slow accumulation of observations about light scattering will become something epochal.

1921

Why Is the Sea Blue?

His first journey overseas. A question formed mid-ocean, tested with a prism borrowed from the ship's kit, would take seven more years to prove — and then change physics forever.

First Voyage

Summer 1921

Oxford & LondonUniversities Congress delegate

Raman sails to England for the first time, representing the University of Calcutta at the Universities Congress and addressing the British Association. In London he visits St Paul's Cathedral and studies the acoustics of its famous Whispering Gallery, co-publishing a paper in Nature that same year.

He meets leading British physicists who already know his name from the journals.

Eureka Moment

Return voyage, 1921

The Mediterranean SeaThe sea becomes an experiment

Sailing home, Raman is transfixed by the deep blue of the Mediterranean. Lord Rayleigh had declared the colour merely a reflection of the sky. Raman reaches into his bag, produces a Nicol prism, and quenches the sky's reflection — the sea grows bluer still.

Water molecules themselves scatter light. He writes a paper before the ship docks. Seven years later this observation will become the Raman Effect.

1924 – 1925

Across the Atlantic to America

A Fellow of the Royal Society — only the fourth Indian ever — Raman tours North America and spends months at Caltech. The network he builds here will carry word of the Raman Effect around the globe within weeks of its discovery.

North America

1924

Toronto, CanadaLight scattering at the British Association

Raman travels to Toronto for the British Association for the Advancement of Science meeting and opens a major discussion on light scattering. The room is full of physicists who will soon be citing him in their own work.

He is elected Fellow of the Royal Society this same year — the fourth Indian to receive the honour.

North America

1924

PhiladelphiaFranklin Institute centenary

At the Franklin Institute centenary Raman meets Robert A. Millikan, the American Nobel Laureate. Millikan is immediately struck by the Indian physicist's range and depth. He extends an invitation to spend time at Caltech in Pasadena.

North America

1924 – 1925

Pasadena, CaliforniaVisiting Professor at Caltech

Four months at the California Institute of Technology as Millikan's guest. Raman lectures, collaborates, and returns with a global network of physicists who are now watching what comes out of the IACS laboratory in Calcutta with keen interest.

His confidence that a Nobel Prize is possible — probable, even — crystallises here in the California sun.

Chapter V · 1928 – 1931

The Nobel Route: Discovery to Stockholm

February 1928: the Raman Effect is proved in a Calcutta laboratory. December 1930: a turbaned Indian physicist stands before King Gustav V and weeps — because India had no flag of its own.

Discovery

28 February 1928

Calcutta — IACS LabA new type of secondary radiation

The conclusive experiment. Raman and K.S. Krishnan observe that a fraction of scattered photons carry a different energy from the incident beam — a phenomenon that will bear his name for ever. A one-page paper is rushed to Nature and published on 31 March 1928.

Within a year, 150 papers cite it. Raman books tickets to Stockholm — in July, four months before the Nobel Committee announces its decision. He knows.

Nobel Route

1929

MysoreConvocation before departure

Before sailing for Europe, Raman delivers a convocation address in Mysore — the city whose maharaja had already conferred on him the title Rajasabha Bhushana, complete with gold-brocaded coat and shawl. He treasures this robe more than any British knighthood.

Nobel Route

1929

Bombay (Mumbai)Setting sail for Europe

Raman and his wife Lokasundari board ship at Bombay's Apollo Bunder, bound for Europe. This is now a Nobel journey in all but the announcement — which will come in November, while he is already at sea, or already in Europe.

Nobel Route

1929

Egypt — Suez & CairoPyramids on the way to the Prize

The ship passes through the Suez Canal. Raman visits the Pyramids at Giza — monuments of human ingenuity that move him deeply. He muses on the ancient Egyptians' knowledge of geometry and optics, seeing in them a kindred obsession with the laws of nature.

Nobel Route

1929

Rome, ItalyMuseums and the Vatican

In Rome, Raman walks the museums, the Vatican galleries, and ancient ruins. He had dreamt of Athens and Rome since a boy in Vizagapatam, when his brother CS described the Greek amphitheatres carved into hillsides, the view of the Ionian Sea from Taormina, and Mount Etna glittering in the light. Now the dream is real.

Nobel Route

1929

GenoaGateway to the north

From Genoa's harbour Raman and Lokasundari travel northward through the continent, the Ligurian coast framing the same blue-green Mediterranean water that had captivated him eight years earlier. The question he had posed on that 1921 return voyage is now the answer that will win the world's greatest science prize.

Nobel Route

1929

Bristol, EnglandFaraday Society address

At the Faraday Society meeting in Bristol, Raman addresses European physicists on light scattering in full — his most comprehensive public exposition of the Raman Effect to date. The room is electrified. Stockholm cannot be far away.

Nobel Prize

10 December 1930

Stockholm, SwedenIndia's first Nobel in science

King Gustav V places the medal in Raman's hands. As the audience applauds, Raman turns and sees the British Union Jack flying above his seat. He breaks down in tears: his country has no flag of its own.

In his banquet speech, Lokasundari notes, he tells the assembled laureates that the greatest obstacle to science in his land is poverty — and the greatest resource is the curiosity of its people. He is forty-two years old. He is the first Asian to win the Nobel Prize in Physics.

Nobel Route

December 1930

Uppsala, SwedenGuest of Siegbahn

After the Stockholm ceremony, Raman travels to Uppsala University as the guest of Professor Manne Siegbahn, the 1924 Nobel laureate in physics. The two men share a deep interest in X-ray spectroscopy and optical phenomena. Raman returns to India carrying the gold medal and the quiet certainty that his country can — and must — become a world scientific power.

1933 – 1956

Bangalore, Diamonds & the Science of India

Raman plants trees on a campus, builds an academy, visits diamond mines, and refuses government money — all from one city he will never leave again.

Institution Builder

1933 – 1948

Bangalore — IIScThe director plants trees

Raman arrives at the Indian Institute of Science as its director. His first act is to order saplings planted across the campus — those trees are now broad avenues. He founds the Indian Academy of Sciences in 1934, launches its journal, and refuses all government grants for his personal research.

He works on crystal lattices, diamond optics, and the physiology of colour vision. Five hundred students at a time. Not a paisa from the state.

Research Journey

1942

Panna, Madhya PradeshInto the diamond mines

Wartime India. Raman travels to Panna's legendary diamond fields in the Vindhyan plateau to study the optical properties of gem diamonds at their source. The trip feeds directly into his research on the lattice vibrations of diamond — the work that will define his Bangalore years.

He believed that diamonds, with their extraordinary optical purity, were nature's finest laboratory instruments. He was not wrong.

Milestone

1948

AllahabadSixtieth birthday & the Science Congress

The Indian Science Congress in Allahabad doubles as a celebration of Raman's sixtieth birthday. Colleagues and former students gather from across the country. He is at this point already retired from IISc and building his own, government-free research institute back in Bangalore.

Domestic Lecture

Mid-1950s

KanpurTeaching a new generation

Raman visits Kanpur for lectures and institutional visits, part of his continuing effort to spread scientific culture beyond the major research cities. He was profoundly committed to the idea that India needed a broad base of researchers — not just a handful of geniuses — to become a scientific power.

International

1956

Lindau, GermanyNobel Laureates convene

Raman attends the Lindau Nobel Laureates Meeting — the annual gathering where laureates meet the next generation of scientists. At sixty-eight, he is one of the most distinguished figures present. He speaks about the unity of scientific inquiry across nations and ideologies, a belief he has held for fifty years.

1958 – 1970

The Lenin Prize Tour & the Final Lectures

One last great international journey — Bombay to Genoa, Sweden, Moscow, Budapest — and then home to Bangalore, where he would plant a papaya tree and refuse to go gently.

Late Honour

1958

BombayThe last great voyage begins

At seventy, Raman boards ship at Bombay for his final international journey — to receive the Lenin Peace Prize in Moscow. His wife Lokasundari is at his side. The route runs westward: Genoa, Sweden, Moscow, Budapest.

Late Honour

1958

Genoa, ItalyEurope again

The ship docks at Genoa — the same harbour from which he had departed northward in 1929. Raman is now a legend. The Italian press covers his arrival; the Indian embassy greets him with ceremony. He is unimpressed by ceremony and impatient to reach Moscow.

Late Honour

1958

Gothenburg, SwedenReunion with his son

In Gothenburg Raman pauses to meet his son Radhakrishnan, who is working in Sweden. A rare family reunion outside India. They walk the harbour, and Raman — ever the scientist — notices the colour of the Baltic sky over the water, still asking the same question he asked in 1921.

Lenin Peace Prize

1958

Moscow, USSRThe Lenin Peace Prize

In the Soviet capital, Raman receives the Lenin Peace Prize and addresses the Soviet Academy of Sciences. He speaks about the responsibility of scientists to resist the use of their discoveries for destruction — a theme that has haunted him since Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Soviet scientists receive him as a hero. He is polite, curious, and refuses to be used as a political symbol by anyone.

Late Honour

1958

Budapest, HungaryHungarian Academy of Sciences

The Hungarian Academy of Sciences honours Raman with a fellowship. Budapest, with its Danube bridges and domed parliament, is his last major European stop. He will not travel internationally again after this journey.

He returns to Bangalore and to the Raman Research Institute, which he runs on its own funds, answerable to no ministry.

Final Lectures

Early 1970

Kanpur & DelhiThe last lecture tour

In the early weeks of 1970, Raman undertakes his final domestic journey — to Kanpur and Delhi — to lecture, to meet students, to insist one more time that Indian science must be free from political interference and industrial pressure.

He returns to the Raman Research Institute in Bangalore. In October he suffers a heart attack in his laboratory. He asks to be moved into the garden, surrounded by his students and his rose bushes.

Legacy

21 November 1970

Raman Research Institute, Bangalore"There will be others to enjoy them."

A few days before his death, Raman plants a papaya tree in the grounds of his institute. "I will not be here when it bears fruit," he says, "but there will be others to enjoy them."

He dies on 21 November 1970, aged 82, in the gardens he loved. By then, 10,000 scientific papers have been published on the Raman Effect. The institute he built without government money continues as a flourishing research centre — his most durable monument.

"A voyage to Europe in the summer of 1921 gave me the first opportunity of observing the wonderful blue opalescence of the Mediterranean Sea."

— C.V. Raman · Nobel Lecture · Stockholm · 11 December 1930
